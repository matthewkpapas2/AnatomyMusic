Why it looks like this

Nothing here is random decoration. Each part of the picture follows one measurement, shown live under each line.

Color → the notes
Each note name has its own hue, placed around the circle of fifths, so a key shows up as a family of neighboring colors. Only notes that are sounding glow.
Light, warmth and vividness → key mode and tempo
Major keys and faster tempos render lighter, warmer and more saturated. Minor and slower music renders darker and cooler. People make the same match between music and color, and it runs through the emotion they hear (Palmer et al., 2013).
Sharp or round → brightness of the sound
Bright, harsh sound (energy high in the spectrum) cuts thin creases and throws angular shards and polygons. Soft, dark sound makes round blobs and circles. Listeners tie harsh timbres to angular shapes and soft ones to rounded shapes (Adeli et al., 2014).
Shockwaves → the beat
Each detected beat launches a ring from the chord. How hard it hits follows punch: how far peaks jump above the average level.
Trails → sustain
Smooth, held notes leave long trails. Short, percussive sound vanishes quickly.
The triangle → the chord
Its corners sit on the chord's three notes around a clock of the 12 notes (C at the top, like the dots around it). Major and minor chords are mirror-image triangles, and the corners glide the shortest way to the next chord, so you can watch the harmony move. With no clear chord, a star shows each note's strength.
Symmetry → repetition
When the song closely repeats something from at least 15 seconds earlier, the picture folds into a kaleidoscope.
Outer geometry → the section
Each section gets its own motif (orbits, lattice, petals, spiral, star, waves). When a section comes back, its motif comes back. During live listening, sections are confirmed about 9 seconds late; replays are exact.
Rushing forward → a build-up
When brightness and loudness climb together for several seconds, streaks fly outward and everything accelerates.
Blackout, then burst → a drop
A sudden silence pulls everything inward. When the sound slams back, it explodes outward in a flash.
Glow at the center → bass
Energy below 250 Hz swells the center in the key's home color.
Sparkles → the airy top end
Energy between 6 and 16 kHz (cymbals, breath, shimmer) makes points of light flicker.
Comet → one clear pitch
When a single note is clear (a voice, a whistle, a solo line), a comet follows it. Its angle is the note and its distance from the center is how high it is.
Grain and turbulence → noisiness
Noisy sound (a flat spectrum, like hiss or distortion) adds grain and stirs the currents.

Research behind two of the mappings: Palmer et al., "Music–color associations are mediated by emotion," PNAS 2013 · Adeli, Rouat & Molotchnikoff, "Audiovisual correspondence between musical timbre and visual shapes," Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 2014.

    How it works

    Everything runs in this page. Sound is split into short overlapping slices (2,048 samples each, about 100 per second), and each slice goes through a Fourier transform to see which frequencies are present.

    Pulse
    Sudden rises in energy ("onsets") are collected over 8 seconds, and the engine checks which beat spacing makes them line up best (autocorrelation, 60–200 BPM). It then looks at how far each real hit lands from a perfectly steady grid. A few milliseconds usually means programmed drums or a click track.
    Harmony
    A longer 8,192-sample transform finds the peaks in the spectrum and folds them into 12 note names (a chromagram). Chords come from matching those 12 values against every major and minor triad. The key comes from comparing the long-term note balance with the Krumhansl–Kessler profiles, which describe how often each note is heard in each key.
    Shape
    Every half second becomes one block described by its notes, tone color and loudness. Each block is compared with every earlier block, which builds the triangle. Sections begin where the sound before and after a point is internally similar but different from each other (Foote's checkerboard novelty). Sections are matched in all 12 transpositions, so a chorus moved up a key is still recognized.
    Moments
    Drops, build-ups, bass entrances and key changes are found by watching loudness, brightness and bass energy for specific shapes over time.

    These are measurements, not certainties. Anything labeled "likely" or "about" is an estimate. Relative keys such as A minor and C major share all their notes, so either reading can be fair.

    Transitions

      Checked but not suggested
        New to DJing? The words used here
        Beatmatch
        Adjust one song's tempo (the pitch fader, or Sync) so both beats line up.
        Downbeat, "the one"
        The first beat of a bar. Changes land hardest here.
        Bar, phrase
        A bar is 4 beats. Songs are built in phrases of 4, 8 or 16 bars, and sections start on phrase lines. Transitions sound right when they start and end on one.
        3-band EQ
        The low, mid and high knobs on each channel. Turned all the way down, a band is "killed". Bass lives in the lows, voices and chords in the mids, hats and cymbals in the highs.
        Bass swap
        Turning one song's low knob down and the other's up in the same moment, so two basslines never play at once.
        Filter
        One knob that removes the lows (high-pass) or the highs (low-pass).
        Key lock
        Keeps a song's pitch the same when you change its tempo.
        Trim / gain
        A song's input level, set before the fader so both songs play equally loud.
        Camelot code
        DJ shorthand for keys (8A is A minor). Neighbors on the wheel mix well.
        Pickup
        Words sung just before a downbeat. Start the song early enough to keep them.
        Build-up, drop
        A build-up takes the bass and kick away and adds tension (risers, snare rolls, a rising filter). The drop is the downbeat where they come back. Every transition here lands on one.
        Echo, reverb wash
        Effects that let a song trail off: repeats of its last beat, or a long tail of reverb with no beat in it.
        Brake, spinback
        Effects that stop a song like a turntable losing power, or throw it into reverse.

        How the Mix tab decides

        Both songs are analyzed in this page by the same engine as the rest of Anatomy, and never leave your computer. The Mix tab then measures what a DJ listens for and checks every idea against the rules that make a transition sound clean.

        Beat grid
        The audio is split into kick, mid and snare bands, and one steady grid of beats is fitted to where their hits land, to within a few milliseconds. The kick decides where the beat is, so off-beat hats and bass lines can't pull the grid half a beat off. Which beat starts each bar is a vote between chord changes (they land on beat 1), the kick on 1 and 3, and the snare or clap on 2 and 4.
        Phrases
        Each bar line gets a score for how much changes there, in harmony, tone, loudness, drums and vocals. Section starts move to the strongest nearby bar line, preferring sections that last whole 4-bar phrases.
        Vocals
        Lead vocals are usually mixed dead center, pitched and syllabic, so Anatomy listens for exactly that, every 23 ms. Centered synth leads look the same, so it says "likely vocals". Mono files can't be checked this way.
        Drops
        Each bar's bass and kick level is measured from the audio. A drop is where they come back at full strength after the song took them away (a breakdown or build-up), or where a riser stops dead and the beat gets fuller. A chorus "hit" is the same thing on a smaller scale, after a one-bar stop. Every transition lands on one of the next song's drops or hits.
        Sound check
        Every idea's plan is played through on paper ten times a second. Rhythm: do the beats lock wherever both songs play? Phrase: are both cue points on phrase lines? One voice: do two singers ever overlap, and does anyone get cut off mid-word? Key: where both songs overlap, do their keys agree? Flow: does the sound sag in the middle, or land quieter than it left? Clean audio: is anything stretched or pitch-shifted hard? Nothing scoring under 70 is suggested (72 at Intermediate, 75 at Advanced, which also need the one-voice part above 45 and 60), so a more creative level is never the worse-sounding one.
        Ranking
        Sound check first (55%), then payoff (45%): how big the drop it lands is, how much tension leads into it, and how much the bass actually jumps at that downbeat in the plan.
        Compatibility
        One number for the pair: tempo, key, whether the next song has a drop to land on, loudness, vocals, and (weighted most) how good the best transition found is. The weights are judgment calls for now.
        Loudness
        Measured in LUFS, the way streaming services do, so the trim advice matches what you hear.
        Previews
        Rendered in your browser: a 3-band kill EQ like a club mixer's, filters, a tempo-synced echo, a reverb, a simple time-stretch and a limiter. Nothing uses stem splitting: an in-page split leaks too much of the rest of the song to sound clean.
